On 12 November, millions of people opened Outlook and nothing loaded.

Ashmita Pandey · Nov 13, 2026 · System Design · Distributed Systems · Cloud Computing

On 12 November, millions of people opened Outlook and nothing loaded. Teams messages wouldn't send. Microsoft 365 apps kept spinning. Even some outside tools that connect to Microsoft stopped working. For some users it went on for more than 20 hours. So what knocked out one of the biggest clouds in the world? Not hackers, and not a sudden rush of users. It started with a few servers that suddenly became unavailable. Normally that's no big deal. If one server goes down, its traffic moves to the others. Think of a supermarket: when one counter closes, those customers join the other queues. But this time, the other counters couldn't keep up. The servers that were still running got all that extra traffic. They became overloaded, started failing too, and pushed even more traffic onto whatever was left. One failure set off the next, and the next. 🧱🧱🧱 This is called a cascading failure. Microsoft restarted servers and moved traffic around. But some servers came back up and went unresponsive again as soon as traffic returned. That's why the fix took so long. The lesson for anyone building software: - Redundancy means having enough backup capacity that losing a few servers doesn't sink the rest. - Throttling means that when a server is overloaded, it accepts only what it can handle and politely says "try again later" to the rest, instead of slowing down for everyone and crashing. - Fault tolerance means planning for "what if several things fail at once?" - Recovery should be gradual. Bring traffic back slowly, not all at once. If this can happen to Microsoft, it can happen to any system. Good engineering isn't about never failing. It's about stopping one failure from taking everything down. Have you ever seen one small failure spread across a system? 👇 #SystemDesign #DistributedSystems #BackendEngineering #CloudComputing
